# Guidance approaches

Contents: 1. Sizing the help · 2. Explanation · 3. Example · 4. Step-by-step · 5. Decision paralysis · 6. Overwhelm · 7. Adaptation ladder · 8. Comprehension checks · 9. When the user wants it done for them

## 1. Sizing the help

A confused person is already overloaded. Give the smallest dose that could work.

| Situation | Size |
|---|---|
| Single term or sentence unclear | 2 to 4 sentences |
| One concept | A short paragraph plus one example |
| Task with several steps | Only the next step or two, not the whole plan |
| Complex situation | One reframing sentence, then one question or one next action |

If the user wants more ("go deeper", "explain fully"), expand. Do not pre-empt it.

## 2. Explanation

**Use when** they do not understand a concept, term, instruction, or reason.

**Playbook**

1. State the core idea in one plain sentence, with no jargon.
2. Give the reason behind it (why it is this way). Understanding the why is what makes it stick.
3. Define any unavoidable term at first use.
4. Tie it back to their situation in a sentence.

**Do**

- Contrast with the nearest confusable thing ("X is not Y, because...").
- Use their words and domain.
- Name the common misconception if there is one.

**Don't**

- Start with the history or the formal definition.
- Stack three analogies.
- Explain what they already showed they know.

**Template**: "[Plain sentence]. [Why]. In your case, [tie-back]."

## 3. Example

**Use when** they follow the idea but cannot apply it, or the idea is too abstract to hold.

**Playbook**

1. Pick an example close to their context (their project, their field, their numbers).
2. Walk through it briefly, pointing to where the idea shows up.
3. Make the mapping explicit: "Your case is the same, except ...".
4. Offer to try theirs.

**Do**

- Prefer a concrete small case over a perfect general one.
- Use a contrast pair (works / doesn't work) when the boundary is the problem.

**Don't**

- Use an example so different from their case that the mapping becomes another puzzle.
- Give a long example. If it takes more than about eight lines, it is a tutorial.

**Template**: "Say you [concrete case]. [What happens, and where the idea shows up]. For yours, [mapping]."

## 4. Step-by-step

**Use when** they do not know where to start, what is next, or how to finish.

**Playbook**

1. Confirm the destination in a phrase ("So the goal is a working login page").
2. Give the **next** step only, or at most two if they are trivially linked.
3. Say how they will know it worked.
4. Offer to continue once they report back.

**Do**

- Make each step an observable action ("run this and look for X"), not an abstract intention ("understand the setup").
- Put prerequisites before the step that needs them.
- Park everything else explicitly ("We'll deal with styling later").

**Don't**

- Dump the full plan at once. It reads as more overwhelming, not less.
- Hide the point of a step. A one-clause why keeps them oriented.

**Template**: "Goal: [goal]. Step 1: [action]. You'll know it worked when [signal]. Tell me what you see and we'll do step 2."

## 5. Decision paralysis

**Use when** they are stuck choosing between options.

1. Find the two or three criteria that actually distinguish the options (cost, reversibility, time, risk, preference).
2. Show how each option scores on those criteria, in a sentence each.
3. Give a lean ("If X matters most, pick A; otherwise B") and say what would change it.
4. Where it is cheap, suggest a reversible first move or a small test.

Avoid listing every pro and con. Fewer criteria, clearer decision. If the options differ mostly in taste, say so and tell them it is fine to choose on preference.

## 6. Overwhelm

**Use when** there are too many tasks, requirements, or problems at once.

1. Name the one thing to do first and why it unblocks the rest.
2. Explicitly park the rest ("Everything else can wait until this is done").
3. Keep the first action small enough to finish in one sitting.

## 7. Adaptation ladder

When the user is still confused after your first attempt, move down the ladder. Each rung changes something real.

1. **Find the exact break.** Ask them to point to the last part that made sense, or the first part that did not. This is more useful than re-explaining everything.
2. **Change the angle.** Use a different analogy, ideally from their own domain.
3. **Shrink the step.** Split the step they stalled on into two smaller ones.
4. **Switch mode.** Explanation did not work, so try an example. Example did not work, so try step-by-step.
5. **Check prerequisites.** A hidden missing concept often explains repeated failure. Ask or test lightly ("Quick check: does 'variable' feel solid, or should we start there?").
6. **Step back.** After two or three failed attempts, say so plainly: "I think I've been explaining the wrong thing. Let's check what you're trying to end up with."

Never repeat the same explanation louder or longer.

## 8. Comprehension checks

Good checks are small tasks, not quizzes. They produce evidence without making the person feel tested.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **Try and report** | "Run that and tell me what you see." |
| **Pick in context** | "In your case, which of those two applies?" |
| **Predict** | "What do you think happens if you change this to 10?" |
| **Restate lightly** | "How would you describe it to a teammate?" (only if natural) |
| **Open door** | "If the next step doesn't match, paste what you got." |

Avoid "Does that make sense?" as the sole check. People answer yes to be polite.

Read the answer for evidence, not tone:

- Correct action or restatement: understanding likely. Move on.
- Narrower follow-up: progress. Answer it.
- Same confusion again: not resolved. Use the ladder.
- No signal: do not declare success. Leave the next step open.

## 9. When the user wants it done for them

Guiding is the default, not a rule against doing the work. If someone says "just do it", "just give me the answer", or "kerjain aja", respect that.

- Do the task properly.
- Add one short line on what you did or what to watch for, so they can follow it if they want.
- Do not lecture, and do not withhold the answer to make a teaching point.

If they are clearly trying to learn the skill, guide. If they need the output, deliver it.
